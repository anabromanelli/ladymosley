Timeline

A chronological account of Lady Diana Mosley's life, family, residences, publications, friendships and major historical events.

1910–1928

Childhood & Early Years

17 June 1910

Birth

Born Diana Freeman-Mitford in Belgravia, London.

1910–1919

Batsford Park

The Mitford family live primarily at Batsford Park, Gloucestershire.

1916–1919

London During the War

The family divides its time between Batsford and temporary London residences during the First World War.

1919

Asthall Manor

The family moves to Asthall Manor, Oxfordshire, which becomes Diana's most formative childhood home.

1922

Life at Asthall Manor

Diana grows up in the close-knit Mitford family environment at Asthall Manor, surrounded by her sisters Nancy, Pamela, Unity, Jessica and Deborah.

1926

Education at Home

Like her sisters, Diana receives a largely private education at home, shaped by governesses, reading, music and the traditions of the English country house.

1928

Coming of Age

As Diana enters adulthood, she becomes increasingly prominent within aristocratic social circles in Britain.

1929–1933

Marriage to Bryan Guinness & London Society

1929

Marriage to Bryan Guinness

Diana marries Bryan Walter Guinness, heir to the Guinness brewing fortune and a member of the Anglo-Irish aristocracy.

1930

Birth of Jonathan Guinness

Diana and Bryan's first child, Jonathan Guinness, is born.

1931

Birth of Desmond Guinness

Their second son, Desmond Guinness, is born.

Early 1930s

Life Among the Bright Young Things

Diana becomes associated with the artistic and aristocratic circles of interwar Britain, alongside writers, politicians and figures of high society.

1932

Meeting Sir Oswald Mosley

Diana meets Sir Oswald Mosley, the former Conservative and Labour Member of Parliament who had founded the New Party and later the British Union of Fascists.

Their relationship develops while both are still married.

1933

Leaving Bryan Guinness

Diana separates from Bryan Guinness. Her relationship with Mosley becomes the subject of considerable public attention.

1934–1939

Marriage to Oswald Mosley & The European Years

1934

Life at Wootton Lodge

Diana joins Oswald Mosley and becomes part of his political and social circle.

1936

Marriage at Joseph Goebbels' Home

Diana and Oswald Mosley marry in Berlin at the home of Dr Joseph Goebbels, with only a small number of guests present.

The marriage takes place while they are visiting Germany for the Berlin Olympics.

1936

Living in Germany

The Mosleys spend increasing amounts of time in Germany, where Diana becomes acquainted with leading figures of the Nazi regime.

1938

Birth of Max Mosley

Their son Max Mosley is born in London.

1939

The Outbreak of War

With the beginning of the Second World War, Diana and Oswald Mosley return to Britain. Their political associations bring increasing public scrutiny.

1940–1945

Internment & Wartime Britain

May 1940

Arrest and Internment

Following the introduction of wartime security measures, Diana and Oswald Mosley are arrested and detained under Defence Regulation 18B.

1940–1943

Holloway Prison

Diana is held at Holloway Prison in London alongside other women interned during the war.

During this period she gives birth to her son Alexander, who remains with her in prison during his early months.

1943

Release from Internment

Diana and Oswald Mosley are released from detention on health grounds, though they remain under restrictions for the remainder of the war.

1945

End of the War

Following the defeat of Germany, the Mosleys leave Britain temporarily and begin a new chapter of life abroad.

1946–1960

France, Ireland & A New Life After Politics

1947

Move to France

The Mosleys settle in France, where they establish a quieter private life away from British public attention.

1950s

La Temple de la Gloire

Diana and Oswald live in France, particularly at their estate near Orsay, south of Paris, which becomes their principal home.

1950s

Social and Literary Circles

Diana continues friendships with writers, artists and figures from European aristocratic and intellectual circles.

1959

Return Visits to Britain

The Mosleys increasingly divide their time between France and Britain, maintaining connections with family and friends.

1961–1979

Later Life, Writing & Public Memory

1960s

A Life Between France and England

Diana continues to divide her time between France and Britain, maintaining friendships across literary, artistic and aristocratic circles.

Her homes become known for their collections, conversation and hospitality.

1960s–1970s

Interest in History and Memoir

Diana begins reflecting more deeply on her own experiences, friendships and the extraordinary historical period through which she lived.

1977

Publication of A Life of Contrasts

Diana publishes her autobiography, A Life of Contrasts, offering her personal account of childhood, society, politics, friendships and wartime experiences.

Late 1970s

Historical Interest

Diana's life continues to attract attention from historians and biographers interested in the aristocracy, twentieth-century Britain and the political movements of the interwar period.

1980–1999

Reflections, Interviews & Later Years

1980s

Interviews and Reminiscences

Diana gives occasional interviews, discussing her memories of twentieth-century society, her family and the many prominent figures she had known.

1980s–1990s

Friendships and Correspondence

She maintains lifelong friendships and correspondence with members of European aristocratic, cultural and political circles.

1990s

Recognition as a Historical Figure

By the final decades of the twentieth century, Diana is increasingly viewed as one of the most distinctive and controversial survivors of the interwar aristocratic world.

1999

Move Towards the End of the Century

Diana remains active socially and intellectually, continuing to receive visitors and engage with her long-standing interests in history, literature and culture.

2000–2003

Final Years & Legacy

2000

Ninetieth Birthday

Diana celebrates her ninetieth birthday, marking a lifetime that had spanned the First World War, the rise and fall of political movements, the Second World War and the transformation of British society.

2000–2002

Later Reflections

In her final years, Diana continues to be interviewed and consulted by writers and historians interested in the twentieth century.

She remains known for her remarkable memory, wit and accounts of the people and places that shaped her life.

11 August 2003

Death

Lady Diana Mosley dies at her home in Paris at the age of 93.

After 2003

Historical Assessment & Continuing Interest

2000s–Present

Biographical Research

Diana Mosley's life continues to be studied by historians and biographers examining aristocracy, politics, gender, social history and the cultural world of twentieth-century Europe.

2007

Publication of Diana Mosley: A Life

Anne de Courcy publishes a major biography of Diana Mosley, examining her upbringing, relationships, political associations and later life.

21st Century

A Figure of Historical Debate

Diana Mosley remains a subject of discussion and research due to the complexity of her personal history, her relationships with major figures of the twentieth century and her association with fascist politics.

Her life continues to raise questions about privilege, loyalty, political conviction, memory and personal responsibility.
